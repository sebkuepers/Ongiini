## Anchor reference — German A0–B1

Reference material for authoring + critiquing German cards. Not
exhaustive — you know much more German than this. These are the
load-bearing pieces beginners hit first.

### Common A0–A1 pitfalls (flag these as PARTIAL in grading, target with cards)

These are the things a beginner gets wrong most often. Card authors
+ critic both prioritise these:

- **Sie vs du.** Sie is formal/plural-you/all-strangers-by-default;
  du is reserved for family, friends, children, fellow students.
  Mixing in one sentence ("Hallo, wie geht es Ihnen?") is
  register-incoherent — `Hallo` is informal so it should pair with
  `du`. A neutral-formal greeting + formal pronoun is `Guten Tag,
  wie geht es Ihnen?`.
- **Capitalising nouns.** German capitalises ALL nouns. `haus`
  is partial, `Haus` is correct.
- **Verb position (V2 in main clauses).** The conjugated verb sits
  in 2nd position. `Heute ich gehe ins Büro` is wrong word order;
  the correct sentence is `Heute gehe ich ins Büro`. This is the
  single most common A0/A1 word-order error.
- **Gendered articles (der/die/das).** Beginners default to one
  article and get it wrong. There's no shortcut — gender is learned
  per-noun. Critic should flag gender_error when a card targets
  noun usage and the learner picks the wrong article.
- **Umlauts + ß.** Canonical forms use `ä ö ü ß`; learners without
  the keyboard often type `ae oe ue ss`. Accept BOTH as correct;
  note the canonical form in feedback.
- **Cases for prepositions.** Some prepositions take accusative
  (durch, für, gegen, ohne, um), some dative (aus, bei, mit, nach,
  seit, von, zu), some both depending on motion vs location (an,
  auf, hinter, in, neben, über, unter, vor, zwischen). At A0 stick
  to the most common dative-takers (mit, von, zu, nach) and the
  two-way ones with simple motion semantics.
- **`möchten` vs `mögen`.** `Ich möchte ein Brötchen` (I would
  like a roll) ≠ `Ich mag Brötchen` (I like rolls). At A0 use
  `möchten` for polite ordering / requests.
- **Negation: `nicht` vs `kein`.** `kein` negates nouns
  (`Ich habe kein Geld`); `nicht` negates verbs / adjectives
  (`Ich gehe nicht`). Common error: `Ich habe nicht Geld`.

### Pronunciation notes (for when audio lands)

- `r` is a uvular trill or fricative — not the English alveolar.
- Final `-e` is pronounced (short schwa, not silent).
- `ch` after a/o/u is a hard fricative (ach-laut); after i/e/ä/ö/ü
  it's a soft fricative (ich-laut).
- `s` before a vowel is voiced (English `z`); elsewhere unvoiced.
- `w` is English `v`; `v` is English `f` (in native words).
- Word stress usually falls on the first syllable of a root word.

## Cultural notes

- Default to `Sie` in any unfamiliar adult setting — shops,
  offices, hotels, restaurants, on the street with strangers.
  Children get `du`. Among peers (university, sports clubs, tech
  workplaces), `du` is common but wait for the other person to
  offer it first ("Wir können uns duzen").
- Punctuality is taken seriously, especially in formal contexts.
- Direct communication style: Germans tend to ask straightforwardly
  what they want; this isn't rudeness. "Können Sie mir helfen?" is
  a normal request, not aggressive.
- Cash is still common in many places; ask "Kann ich mit Karte
  bezahlen?" before ordering if you don't have cash.
